TopicSugar and blood sugar — and the habits of a scientist: observing, asking why, fair tests, data.
StoryEight short chapters, about 1,300 words — roughly 15 minutes read aloud.
Back matterLumi’s Secret Lab Notes (pp. 36–45) · Think Like a Scientist (pp. 46–47) · Lumi’s Word Lab, 17 words (pp. 48–57) · Jiwoo’s “Prove It” Tracker (pp. 58–63) · For the Grown-Up Lab Partner (pp. 64–66)

Big ideas

  • Bodies respond to food — and responses differ from person to person.
  • When, how much and with what can matter as much as what.
  • Scientists observe before they decide. “No change” is a real result.
  • A fair test changes one thing at a time.
  • Labels and ads make promises; tests check them.

How this book handles food

  • No food is called good or bad, and there are no calorie or sugar-gram counts. Please keep it that way in class.
  • No one’s body is discussed. Keep comments on feelings and energy, never on size or weight.
  • Don’t collect, display or grade students’ food records. The tracker belongs to the child and stays at home.
  • Plan activities without food or drink, and don’t use sweets as rewards during the unit.
  • Praise “I didn’t feel anything” and “I don’t know.” Let a wrong reason stand for a minute; ask one question instead of correcting it.

Sensitivity notes

Diabetes

Some students live with type 1 diabetes and take insulin. Follow their care plan and their family’s lead, and never ask them to explain their condition to the class. If it comes up, a simple, accurate line: “Some people’s bodies don’t make enough insulin, so they take it as medicine. It’s the same helper.”

Food access and allergies

Not every student eats breakfast every day. Present “food before the drink” as one of Lumi’s ideas to test, not a rule. Keep every activity food-free.

Body talk

If a student comments on someone’s body, redirect to what bodies do: “In this book we’re scientists of how bodies feel and work.”

Three short lessons

Lesson 1 · Read & Notice 40 min

5 minCover prediction: “What is this bottle promising?” Record predictions on the board.
15 minRead aloud. Stop after Chapter 3 (predict), Chapter 5 (what changed?) and Chapter 8 (Jiwoo’s three lessons).
10 minTalk: choose two or three discussion questions.
7 minIntroduce Think Like a Scientist (p. 46): Notice · Wonder · Test · Report.
3 minExit ticket: one thing I noticed, one thing I wonder.

Lesson 2 · Model It 40 min

10 minRe-read Lab Notes 1–3 (pp. 39–41): the roller coaster, the Insulin Crew, two grabbers.
20 minActivity: the Insulin Crew Relay (active) or Draw the Roller Coaster (seated).
10 minDebrief: explain “two grabbers” in your own words. Vocabulary: glucose, insulin, pancreas, energy.

Lesson 3 · Test It 40 min

15 minActivity: Four Things Were True at Once (card sort).
15 minData detectives: analyse Jiwoo’s filled-in week (pp. 61–62) with the questions below.
10 minWrite a fair test on paper. Optional: send home the blank tracker with the family letter.

Data detectives — Jiwoo’s week

Jiwoo’s week (pages 61–62) is made-up data, which makes it safe to analyse together — no student shares their own food. Ask the questions in order; open each one for the teacher note.

1. On how many days did Jiwoo have something sweet?

Teacher note: Six — Day 6 is blank. She only noticed because she wrote it down (p. 62).

2. On which days did she feel nothing?

Teacher note: Days 2, 3 and 5. Three of six logged days show nothing — on purpose (p. 65).

3. What was different about Day 1?

Teacher note: A lot of sugar, all at once, on an empty stomach, right before running — the four things on p. 42.

4. On Days 2 and 3 she had something sweet with or after food and felt nothing. Does that prove food stops a crash?

Teacher note: Not yet — one kid, a few days. It’s a pattern worth testing, one change at a time.

5. Day 4 is an open case: was the homework fog a sugar crash, or the Ice Age? Why is writing down both smart?

Teacher note: A scientist records every possible explanation, then tests one. (The Ice Age is a joke — ask students how they’d rule it out.)

6. What would Jiwoo need to do to be more sure?

Teacher note: More days, and change one thing at a time while keeping the rest the same.

Science background for teachers

Blood sugar has a range. Glucose from carbohydrates enters the blood after eating. The pancreas releases insulin, which signals muscle, fat and liver cells to take glucose in; when blood sugar falls, a second hormone, glucagon, helps release stored glucose. The Insulin Crew is a model of the first half — insulin is a signal, not a carrier, as Lumi admits on page 40.

Two grabbers. Working muscles take glucose from the blood by a route that doesn’t need insulin. Insulin and exercise together clear glucose faster than either alone — the idea behind Lumi’s “two grabbers at once.”

The dip happens to some, not all. In studies of trained adult athletes, a large sugary drink 30–60 minutes before exercise lowered blood sugar early in exercise in some people but not others, and feeling weak rarely matched a measured low. There are no comparable studies in healthy children. Jiwoo’s day is one case — which is why the book asks children to observe rather than predict.

Food first. Fat, protein and fibre slow how fast the stomach empties, so the same carbohydrate eaten with or after food raises blood sugar more gently (studies in adults).

When it’s more than a dip. Low blood sugar that needs treatment is rare in children without diabetes. Shakiness, sweating, dizziness or confusion that keep coming back are a reason to call the pediatrician — as is much more thirst and urination with tiredness or weight loss, a warning sign of type 1 diabetes.

Sugar and behaviour. Controlled studies have not found that sugar changes children’s behaviour or thinking, but adults who expect a sugar effect tend to see one — one more reason to ask children what they noticed instead of telling them.

Family letter

To send home with the blank Sweet Stuff tracker. Copy it onto school letterhead, or print this version.

Dear families,

This week our class read The Day I Tried to Run on Pure Sparkles, a story about a soccer player who learns to observe her own body like a scientist.

Your child may bring home a one-week Sweet Stuff Experiment. It is optional and private: nothing is collected, checked or graded. It asks your child to notice when they have something sweet, and how their body feels 30 minutes later — and it treats “I felt nothing” as a real result.

If you’d like to help: ask, don’t check. Praise “I didn’t feel anything.” Don’t say the finding first. And stop after one week.

The book never calls a food good or bad and includes no calorie or sugar counts; we’re keeping it that way. If you have questions about your child’s health, please talk with your pediatrician.
