What the evidence does — and doesn’t — show

  • The “wet spaghetti” dip: in studies of adult athletes, a big sugary drink 30–60 minutes before exercise lowered blood sugar early in exercise in some people and not in others, and feeling weak rarely matched a measured low. We found no studies in healthy children. Jiwoo’s day is one kid’s case — Lumi’s sample size is one.
  • Food first: fat, protein and fibre slow how fast the stomach empties, so the same sugar raises blood sugar more gently. The studies are in adults.
  • Feeling low: low blood sugar that needs a doctor is rare in children without diabetes. The safety box on the Trackers page says when to call.
  • Sugar and behaviour: controlled studies haven’t found that sugar changes children’s behaviour or thinking — but adults who expect it tend to see it.

Corrections to Lumi’s Word Lab

Three entries in Lumi’s Word Lab simplify too far. Here is what each one says in the book, and what it should say. The rest of the Word Lab stands.

CARBOHYDRATE page 53

In the book: Some carbs break down slowly (like rice), others break down fast (like juice).

It should say: Some carbs raise your blood sugar slowly (like beans or apples). Others raise it fast (like white bread or white rice).

In the international glycemic index tables, white rice is a fast carb, and orange juice and apple juice are slower ones — the book’s example was backwards. Sources 23, 24 below.

GLUCOSE page 54

In the book: Almost everything you eat eventually becomes glucose before your body burns it.

It should say: Your body turns most carbs into glucose, a sugar it burns for energy. It can burn fat for energy too, and it uses protein mostly to build and fix your body.

Fat is burned directly — only a small part of it can become glucose — and protein is used mostly for building. Source 2 below.

CELL page 57

In the book: …trillions of them, all needing glucose to survive.

It should say: …trillions of them. Every cell needs fuel. Many can burn fat too, but some, like red blood cells, need glucose.

“Trillions” is right — about 17 trillion in a 10-year-old. But heart and muscle cells, among others, also burn fat; red blood cells depend on glucose. Sources 2, 25, 26 below.

  2. Jentjens RLPG, Jeukendrup AE. Prevalence of hypoglycemia following pre-exercise carbohydrate ingestion is not accompanied by higher insulin sensitivity. Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab. 2002;12(4):398-413. doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.12.4.398Supports the idea that bodies respond differently: after the same sugary drink, half of 20 trained athletes dipped low and half did not.   3. Marathe CS, Rayner CK, Jones KL, Horowitz M. Relationships between gastric emptying, postprandial glycemia, and incretin hormones.   4. Sun L, Goh HJ, Govindharajulu P, Leow MK, Henry CJ. Postprandial glucose, insulin and incretin responses differ by test meal macronutrient ingestion sequence (PATTERN study). Your hungry brain

  1. Raichle ME, Gusnard DA. Appraising the brain's energy budget. Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A. 2002;99(16):10237-10239. doi.org/10.1073/pnas.172399499Supports the fact that an adult’s brain is about 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of the body’s energy.
  2. Kuzawa CW, Chugani HT, Grossman LI, et al. Metabolic costs and evolutionary implications of human brain development. Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A. 2014;111(36):13010-13015. doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1323099111Supports the fact that children’s brains use an even bigger share: at the peak in childhood, glucose equal to about two-thirds of the body’s resting energy.

Sugar and behaviour

  1. Wolraich ML, Wilson DB, White JW. The effect of sugar on behavior or cognition in children: a meta-analysis. JAMA. 1995;274(20):1617-1621. doi.org/10.1001/jama.1995.03530200053037Supports the finding that sugar did not change children’s behaviour or thinking in 23 carefully controlled studies, though effects in a few children could not be ruled out.
  2. Hoover DW, Milich R. Effects of sugar ingestion expectancies on mother-child interactions. J Abnorm Child Psychol. 1994;22(4):501-515. doi.org/10.1007/BF02168088Supports the point that what parents expect shapes what they see: mothers told their sons had sugar rated them more hyperactive, even though every boy got a sugar-free placebo. 35 boys aged 5–7.
