What the evidence does — and doesn’t — show

  • Caffeine hides sleepiness; it doesn’t take it away. Caffeine sits in the spots where adenosine, a “time to rest” chemical, would go, so the sleepy message gets through less. The body’s need for sleep keeps building, and only sleep clears it.
  • Chocolate has caffeine, but only a little. Dark chocolate and cocoa have more than milk chocolate. Chocolate is still young children’s main source of caffeine in many countries, because children eat it more often than they drink coffee or cola.
  • When you have it matters, and so does how much. Caffeine stays in the body for hours, so caffeine near bedtime is more likely to disturb sleep. Bright light in the evening also holds back melatonin, and children’s melatonin reacts to light more strongly than adults'.
  • Bodies really do differ. People vary a lot in how strongly caffeine affects them and how fast they clear it.
  • What nobody has tested yet. We found no study of whether chocolate at night changes young children’s sleep. Most timing studies gave adults far more caffeine than a dessert holds, and studies of children’s sleep mostly show links, not causes. That makes a child’s own careful notes, including “nothing happened,” genuinely new information.

Corrections to the Lab Notes and Word Lab

Nine passages in Lumi’s Lab Notes and Word Lab simplify too far, or say something the evidence doesn’t support. Here is what each one says in the book, and what it should say. The rest stands.

RECEPTOR page 59

In the book: A special spot on a brain cell shaped for exactly one chemical to fit into it — like a lock waiting for its key.

It should say: A special spot on a cell, shaped so one kind of chemical fits into it, like a key in a lock. But a look-alike chemical can sometimes squeeze in too. That’s what caffeine does.

Caffeine works because a look-alike chemical can fit the same spot, so a receptor isn’t made for just one chemical. Sources 7, 9, 10 below.

Lab Notes 3 (aside) page 43

In the book: (The chair is not really a chair. It’s a receptor — a spot on a brain cell shaped for exactly one chemical. I just like the chairs.)

It should say: (The chair is not really a chair. It’s a receptor — a spot on a brain cell shaped for adenosine. Caffeine is a look-alike that fits too. I just like the chairs.)

The note says the chair fits just one chemical, but the same page shows caffeine sitting in it. Sources 7, 9, 10 below.

Lab Notes 4 page 44

In the book: The chair BLOCKS a message. The factory never MAKES one. Two different jobs — and that night, both stopped.

It should say: The chair BLOCKS a message. The factory sends its message LATE. Two different jobs — and that night, both went wrong.

Bright light and caffeine can make melatonin arrive later or weaker, but they don’t switch it off. Sources 13, 14, 15, 16 below.

Lab Notes 2 (graph) page 42

In the book: [Drawing: the pile keeps rising after the friends fall asleep and is highest at 7 A.M.] Jiwoo’s pile never went anywhere. It just couldn’t get through — so it all waited for morning.

It should say: [Drawing: the pile peaks when Jiwoo finally falls asleep, then slopes partly down by 7 A.M.] Jiwoo’s pile never went anywhere. It just couldn’t get through. Only sleep makes the pile shrink — and she didn’t get enough. So lots of it was still there in the morning.

Sleep is what shrinks the sleepiness pile, so the drawing shouldn’t show it still growing once Jiwoo is asleep. Sources 7, 8 below.

MILLIGRAM page 58

In the book: A tiny unit of weight used to measure very small amounts of things like caffeine or medicine. A milligram is so small you can’t see it, but your body definitely notices it.

It should say: A tiny unit of weight. A thousand milligrams make one gram. We use milligrams to measure small amounts of strong things, like caffeine or medicine, because even a small amount can make a difference in your body.

Bodies react differently, so not every body notices a small amount, which the book’s own grown-up page also says. Sources 4, 19, 22, 35 below.

INSOMNIA page 61

In the book: … On a normal night, insomnia is rare for kids.

It should say: … Lots of kids have trouble sleeping sometimes. If it keeps happening, tell a grown-up. A doctor can help.

Trouble sleeping is common in children, and calling it rare could keep a parent from getting help. Sources 26, 27, 28 below.

TIMING page 62

In the book: When you eat or drink something matters just as much as what you eat. Caffeine at breakfast? Your body handles it fine. Caffeine at dinner? Your sleep gets complicated.

It should say: When you eat or drink something. For caffeine, timing matters a lot. Caffeine early in the day has hours to wear off before bed. Caffeine close to bedtime is still working when you try to sleep.

Pediatricians advise children to avoid caffeine, so the glossary shouldn’t tell them it’s fine at breakfast. Sources 3, 17, 22, 24, 29 below.

CAFFEINE (Lumi’s note) page 56

In the book: Chocolate has caffeine in it — the same stimulant in coffee and energy drinks. Not as much as those, but enough for a nine-year-old body to notice.

It should say: Chocolate has caffeine in it — the same stimulant in coffee and energy drinks. Not as much as those, but it can be enough for some nine-year-old bodies to notice.

We found no study testing whether the caffeine in chocolate is enough for children to feel, and bodies differ. Sources 4, 6, 22 below.

WIND-DOWN (Lumi’s note) page 63

In the book: Melatonin factory workers need dark and quiet to do their job. Ghost stories until midnight didn’t help them either.

It should say: Melatonin factory workers need the dark. Your brain needs calm. Ghost stories until midnight didn’t help either.

Melatonin responds to light and dark rather than noise, though a calm, quiet bedtime still helps sleep. Sources 11, 12, 13, 29, 30 below.

The sleepiness pile and the sleepiness chair

  1. Brain basics: understanding sleep. National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke. Reviewed February 25, 2025.   4. Spielman RM, Dumper K, Jenkins W, Lacombe A, Lovett M, Perlmutter M. Cells of the nervous system. In: Psychology. OpenStax; 2014: section 3.2. Supports the lock-and-key idea of receptors and explains how a blocker can sit in a receptor without switching it on, which is how caffeine takes adenosine’s chair.

Pharmacol Rev. 2018;70(2):384-411. doi.org/10.1124/pr.117.014407Supports “different isn’t wrong”: genes, age, sex and other factors change how fast people clear caffeine and how it affects their sleep.   2. Temple JL, Ziegler AM, Graczyk A, Bendlin A, Sion T, Vattana K. Cardiovascular responses to caffeine by gender and pubertal stage. Caffeine and children’s sleep

  1. Watson EJ, Banks S, Coates AM, Kohler MJ. The relationship between caffeine, sleep, and behavior in children. J Clin Sleep Med. 2017;13(4):533-543. doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.6536Supports a link between caffeine and children’s sleep: children who had more caffeine reported more restless sleep and morning tiredness.
