For the grown-up lab partner

How to be a lab partner

Every book in the series ends with three pages written for you, For the Grown-Up Lab Partner. This page collects the short version from all three books — plus each book’s safety notes.

1 · Ask. Don’t check.

The record belongs to your child. An inspected notebook stops being honest by about day three.

2 · The one that matters

Each book has one rule it calls the one that matters — and each teaches a different honest answer. Read all three books and your child has practised all three.

BOOK 1 · SUGAR AND BLOOD SUGAR

Praise “I didn’t feel anything.”

A child who reports no change has produced an honest result — harder and rarer than the expected one. Three of Jiwoo’s six logged days show nothing, on purpose. Answer that with “Well, sugar is still bad for you,” and everything the story taught comes apart in one sentence. Answer this instead: “Good — you wrote down what actually happened. That’s the whole job.”

The Day I Tried to Run on Pure Sparkles, pages 64–66

BOOK 2 · CAFFEINE AND SLEEP

Praise the question mark.

A child who writes “I don’t know” has produced an honest result — harder and rarer than a confident one. Two of Jiwoo’s five entries are question marks. Answer that with “Well, chocolate still keeps you up,” and everything the story taught comes apart in one sentence. Answer this instead: “Good — you wrote down what you actually knew. That’s the whole job.”

The Sleepover Chocolate Disaster, pages 70–72

BOOK 3 · SALT, WATER, AND HOW YOUR BODY ASKS

Praise the blank day.

A child who writes nothing has produced an honest result — harder and rarer than a full row. Three of Jiwoo’s seven days are blank, on purpose. Answer that with “Well, too much salt is still bad for you,” and everything the story taught comes apart in one sentence. Answer this instead: “Good — you left it empty because it was empty. That’s the whole job.”

The Bottomless Popcorn Paradox, pages 72–74

3 · Let them find it

Don’t say the finding first. Most children notice something unexpected. Name it first, and they confirm your answer instead of finding their own. (Books 1 and 2)

Let a wrong reason stand for a minute. Most children will name a cause — usually the loudest one. Correct it and you hand over an answer, not a method. (Book 3)

Know when to stop

  • Book 1: One week, sweet things only. If it becomes a log of everything eaten, or your child asks if a number is too high, close it and come back in a year or two. Curiosity doesn’t need a streak.
  • Book 2: One week, caffeine only. If it becomes a log of everything eaten, or bedtime becomes a worry, close it and come back in a year or two. Curiosity doesn’t need a streak.
  • Book 3: One week, salty things only. If it becomes a log of everything eaten, or your child asks if a number is too high, close it and come back in a year or two. Curiosity doesn’t need a streak.

Why there are no numbers

Nothing here — and nothing in the books — counts calories or grams, sorts foods into good and bad, or sets rules that begin with “never.” In its 2016 clinical report on preventing obesity and eating disorders, the American Academy of Pediatrics advises against dieting and against framing food or bodies in terms of weight. So the series teaches one verb: notice.

When to see a doctor instead

Each book’s own safety box, word for word.

Book 1 · The Day I Tried to Run on Pure Sparkles

This book is about ordinary, passing sensations. Call your pediatrician if shakiness, dizziness, sweating or headaches keep coming back; if thirst and trips to the bathroom rise sharply, alongside unusual tiredness or weight loss; or after any confusion or fainting. If your child already lives with a diagnosed condition, talk with their care team before running the tracker. Nothing in this book is medical advice.

Book 2 · The Sleepover Chocolate Disaster

This book is about one ordinary night. Talk with your pediatrician before running the tracker if your child has trouble falling asleep, has ever had a racing-heart episode, or lives with a diagnosed condition — for a racing heart, skip the evening chocolate rather than time it. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises children skip energy drinks entirely; page 66 says so in a child’s words. Nothing here is medical advice.

Book 3 · The Bottomless Popcorn Paradox

This book is about ordinary, passing sensations. Call your pediatrician if headaches come daily, wake your child from sleep, or keep getting worse; if thirst and bathroom trips rise sharply alongside unusual tiredness or weight loss; or if your child already lives with a diagnosed condition. Go now for a sudden severe headache with confusion, vomiting or a stiff neck. Nothing here is medical advice.

What this site does — and doesn’t

  • Every printable is free to print and copy for home and classroom use.
  • Nothing here is medical advice. If you have questions about your child’s health, talk with your pediatrician.
