TopicSalt, thirst and water — how a body asks for what it needs — and the habits of a scientist: noticing, checking a reason, fair tests, data.
StoryEight short chapters, about 1,700 words — roughly 20 minutes read aloud.
Back matterLumi’s Secret Lab Notes (pp. 38–49) · Think Like a Scientist (pp. 50–51) · Lumi’s Word Lab, 22 words (pp. 53–65) · Jiwoo’s “Prove It” Tracker (pp. 66–71) · For the Grown-Up Lab Partner (pp. 72–74)

Big ideas

  • Bodies send signals — a dry mouth, a tight head — and a signal can be noticed or missed.
  • Blood keeps a just-right mix of salt and water. Thirst is the body asking for water to balance it.
  • Noticing is a skill. Some bells are loud, some are quiet, and every scientist can practice listening.
  • The first reason you notice is not always the right one. Scientists check a reason before they believe it.
  • A blank day and a question mark are real data.

How this book handles food

  • No food is called good or bad, and there are no sodium or calorie counts. Please keep it that way in class.
  • No one’s body is discussed. Keep comments on feelings and signals, never on size or weight.
  • Don’t collect, display or grade students’ food records. The tracker belongs to the child and stays at home.
  • Plan activities without food or drink, and don’t use snacks as rewards during the unit.
  • Praise a blank day and a question mark. Let a wrong reason stand for a minute; ask one question instead of correcting it.

Sensitivity notes

Salt and fluid plans

Some students follow a salt or fluid plan from their doctor — less salt for some kidney or heart conditions, extra salt for others, such as cystic fibrosis. Follow their care plan and their family’s lead, and never ask them to explain it to the class.

Water and bathroom breaks

This story turns on thirst and a bathroom emergency. Some students need water or the bathroom more often for medical reasons, and some feel embarrassed about it. Make both easy to get, keep the talk on Jiwoo, and never comment on a particular child’s trips.

Headaches

Some students live with headaches or migraines that have nothing to do with salt or water. Treat Jiwoo’s headache as one kid’s case — the book “never promises a headache” (p. 74) — and point families to the doctor box on the Trackers page.

Food at home

Ramen, pizza, chips and pretzels — foods from Jiwoo’s week and the rules page — are everyday food in many homes and cultures, and not every child has snacks to track. Nothing in the book calls a food bad, and a blank day is a real result. Keep every activity food-free.

Different bells

Some children notice body signals early and some late, for many reasons. Use the book’s own line, “Neither bell is broken,” and never point out a particular child’s bell.

Body talk

If a student comments on someone’s body, redirect to what bodies do: “In this book we’re scientists of how bodies feel and work.”

Three short lessons

15 minData detectives: analyse Jiwoo’s filled-in week (pp. 69–70) with the questions below.
10 minWrite a fair test on paper — or check Jiwoo’s new reason with Lumi’s four questions (p. 47). Optional: send home the blank tracker with the family letter.

Data detectives — Jiwoo’s week

Jiwoo’s week (pages 69–70) is made-up data, which makes it safe to analyse together — no student shares their own food. Ask the questions in order; open each one for the teacher note.

1. On how many days did Jiwoo write something down?

Teacher note: Four — Days 1, 4, 5 and 6. Days 2, 3 and 7 are blank, on purpose (p. 73): a blank day is a real result.

2. On which two days did she drink water zero times? What did she notice each time?

Teacher note: Day 1, the popcorn bucket: dry mouth and a tight-hat headache — noticed only when Maya asked. Day 5, pretzels and string cheese (she forgot): mouth a little dry by dinner.

3. On Days 4 and 6 she drank water while she ate, and felt fine. Does that prove water keeps the dry mouth and headache away?

Teacher note: Not yet — one kid, a few days. Look closer: Day 4 came after soccer practice, and on Day 6 Maya passed the pitcher — Jiwoo didn’t ask for it. It’s a pattern worth testing, one change at a time.

4. Day 5 is an open case: thirst, or “just… Wednesday”? Why is writing down both smart?

Teacher note: A scientist records every possible explanation, then checks one. (“Just Wednesday” is a joke — ask students how she could rule it out.)

5. In Jiwoo’s own words (p. 70), what made the popcorn day different?

Teacher note: “Salty AND ninety minutes AND a movie so good I forgot I had a body.” Her tracker on p. 69 adds a fourth: water 0 times in 90 minutes.

6. Jiwoo ends her week with two maybes. What are they — and what would she need to do to find out which is true?

Teacher note: “Maybe the bell was always ringing at the same volume. Maybe I’m just getting better at picking up the phone.” More weeks, the same notes, one change at a time. Lumi’s note on p. 48 says listening can be practiced.

Science background for teachers

Blood keeps a steady mix. Sodium is the main salt in the fluid around our cells, and the body keeps the blood’s saltiness within a narrow range. Special cells in the hypothalamus sense when blood gets saltier; the brain then switches on thirst and releases a hormone, ADH, that tells the kidneys to save water.

A salty snack tips the mix a little. In a study of adults, one salty meal nudged blood sodium up slightly. Water moves by osmosis out of cells toward the saltier fluid around them, so cells lose a little water: far less than the drawing on page 42 shows. Blood is never salt-free, which is why Lumi’s graph on page 41 is best read as “salt I ate.”

Thirst is the bell; a headache is a different clue. Thirst is the urge to drink, often with a dry mouth. Going without water can bring on a headache in some people, but researchers say the mechanism isn’t fully understood, and the international headache classification has no separate “dehydration headache.” Headaches have many causes, which is why the corrections keep the two apart.

The bell rings early. A 2002 review about adults concluded that thirst starts before the body is short enough of water to count as dehydrated. Experts draw that line in different places, and the review left out hard exercise and hot weather. People differ in how readily they feel thirsty; we found no studies of this in children.

Attention turns body signals down, not off. What we notice depends on where our attention is. People who listened to outside sounds while exercising reported less tiredness, and distraction moderately reduces children’s pain during shots and other needle procedures. Sensing the inside of your body is called interoception; in adults, months of practice brought small improvements.

One cause, or several? Kidneys make urine at a fairly steady rate, and the bladder stores it until it is emptied. Jiwoo’s bathroom emergency was probably a mix of the water after soccer, a long film and skipping the bathroom beforehand (page 8). In rested adults, most of the extra urine from a big drink comes within a couple of hours, so timing matters too. “What else was true that day?” is the right question.

Family letter

To send home with the blank Salty-Snack Experiment tracker. Copy it onto school letterhead, or print this version.

Dear families,

This week our class read The Bottomless Popcorn Paradox, a story about a girl who learns to hear her body’s signals — and to check her own reasons — like a scientist.

Your child may bring home a one-week Salty-Snack Experiment. It is optional and private: nothing is collected, checked or graded. It asks your child to notice when they have something salty, how many times they drink water, and how their mouth and head feel — and it treats a blank day and a question mark as real results.

If you’d like to help: ask, don’t check. Praise the blank day. If your child names a reason that sounds wrong, let it stand for a minute and ask one question. And stop after one week.

The book never calls a food good or bad and includes no sodium or calorie counts; we’re keeping it that way. If you have questions about your child’s health, please talk with your pediatrician.
